Functional Question 2: NR1 Ligandability Analysis

Free-energy (ΔG) analysis Analysis of NR1

Author: Vallari Eastman
Date: Nov 2025

Formal Functional Question 2:

How can free-energy (ΔG) analysis be used to evaluate the energetic plausibility of antibody epitopes and identify conformational states of NR1 that are ligandable?

Introduction:

Known epitopes tend to lie in low energetic zones. Proteins often have “frustration landscapes” (energy maps) where surface-exposed, energetically neutral patches serve as natural ligand-binding sites (including for antibodies). Epitopes generally avoid residues with high local energetic frustration because antibodies cannot bind tightly to regions that are structurally strained or mechanically load-bearing.

Epitopes almost always occupy residues with low ΔΔG sensitivity. Mutation scanning studies (alanine scans, saturation mutagenesis) show that epitope residues are typically tolerant to mutation, meaning their ΔΔG of folding is small or near zero. This is because antibody binding cannot rely on residues that are crucial for the protein’s structural integrity. Those residues simply cannot move or deform to accommodate an antibody.

Tajima et al. (2022) identifies several epitope locations on the NR1 amino-terminal domain (ATD). Mutation of E101 removes antibody binding, showing that this residue is part of the epitope (Tajima et al., 2022). Changes to the N368 glycosylation site also reduce antibody effects, placing another part of the epitope near this loop (Tajima et al., 2022). Structural modeling shows that these regions form a contiguous surface on the upper ATD that antibodies recognize (Tajima et al., 2022). Overall, the mapped epitope includes the E101 loop, the N368-associated loop, and nearby exposed ATD surface.

Methods:

Thermal Stability Prediction Work Flow

0. Previously in VL07, Cloned pretrained protein sequence embedding repo and download its pretrained models.
1. Generate single point mutations based on epitope location from Tajima et al., 2022
2. Load mutations list into VL02 and generate ΔΔG (ddG) values for single-point mutants from FireProt.
3. Load ddG values into current notebook and group by epitope for analysis
6. Evaluate using grouped cross-validation across proteins and summarize with Pearson R.

Assumptions / limitations:
a. Each ddG measurement is high quality and comparable across proteins (not always true experimentally).
b. Single point mutations behave independently (ignores epistasis and environment).
c. ddG is treated as a purely sequence-driven function (but stability is super contextual — solvent, pH, folding helpers, etc.).

# alanine scan and charge swap

import pandas as pd

def mutate_sequence(seq: str, pos0: int, new_aa: str) -> str:
    seq_list = list(seq)
    seq_list[pos0] = new_aa
    return "".join(seq_list)

# --- Alanine scan ---
alanine_mutants = []

for epitope_name, positions in epitopes.items():
    for pos0 in positions:
        wt_aa = nr1_seq[pos0]
        if wt_aa == "A":
            continue  # already alanine

        mut_seq = mutate_sequence(nr1_seq, pos0, "A")

        alanine_mutants.append({
            "epitope_name": epitope_name,
            "mutation_type": "ALA_scan",
            "pos_index": pos0 + 1,           # 1-based
            "pos_0based": pos0,
            "wt_aa": wt_aa,
            "mut_aa": "A",
            "mutation_label": f"{wt_aa}{pos0+1}A",
            "wt_sequence": nr1_seq,
            "mut_sequence": mut_seq,
        })

ala_df = pd.DataFrame(alanine_mutants)

# --- Charge swaps ---
charge_swaps = {"K": "E", "R": "E", "H": "E", "D": "K", "E": "K"}

def get_charge_swap(wt_aa: str):
    return charge_swaps.get(wt_aa, None)

charge_mutants = []

for epitope_name, positions in epitopes.items():
    for pos0 in positions:
        wt_aa = nr1_seq[pos0]
        mut_aa = get_charge_swap(wt_aa)
        if mut_aa is None:
            continue  # not a charged residue we want to swap

        mut_seq = mutate_sequence(nr1_seq, pos0, mut_aa)

        charge_mutants.append({
            "epitope_name": epitope_name,
            "mutation_type": "charge_swap",
            "pos_index": pos0 + 1,
            "pos_0based": pos0,
            "wt_aa": wt_aa,
            "mut_aa": mut_aa,
            "mutation_label": f"{wt_aa}{pos0+1}{mut_aa}",
            "wt_sequence": nr1_seq,
            "mut_sequence": mut_seq,
        })

charge_df = pd.DataFrame(charge_mutants)

mutants_df = pd.concat([ala_df, charge_df], ignore_index=True)

Results

Predicted ΔΔG values ranged from –0.9 to +2.9 kcal/mol, showing that the two epitope regions differ noticeably in how sensitive they are to mutation. The E101 loop showed the broadest range, from slightly stabilizing to strongly destabilizing changes. Several residues landed in the +1 to +2 kcal/mol range, and a few exceeded +2 kcal/mol, suggesting that some positions in this loop play a bigger role in maintaining local structure than others.

The N368 loop showed a tighter but consistently positive set of ΔΔG values, mostly between 0 and +2.8 kcal/mol. This pattern fits with what is known about the loop’s structural role around the N368 glycosylation site—mutations here tend to disrupt stability rather than improve it. As expected, charge-swapping mutations produced the largest ΔΔG shifts in both regions, while alanine substitutions resulted in a wider spread of effects.

When ΔΔG values were plotted along the sequence, the two epitope windows formed clear clusters. Both clusters included several high-ΔΔG positions, and neither region appeared mutation-tolerant across the board. Overall, the patterns point to localized energetic sensitivity within each loop, which is consistent with how functional epitope surfaces typically behave.

Analysis

The ΔΔG patterns across both loops point toward a shared theme: neither region behaves like a flexible, unimportant surface. Instead, both show signs of local structural constraint, which is exactly what would be expected for functional antibody epitopes on the NR1 ATD. In other words, these loops are not just loose decorations on the protein surface. They appear to contribute to how the ATD is shaped and stabilized.

The N368 loop stands out because nearly every mutation increases ΔΔG to some degree. This consistent destabilization fits well with its known biological role. The N368 site carries an N-linked glycan, and multiple studies have shown that this glycan helps stabilize the ATD fold and influences antibody binding. Seeing uniformly positive ΔΔG values here suggests that even small changes disrupt the local environment, reinforcing the idea that this region is structurally important and not particularly tolerant of mutation. That kind of intolerance tends to show up in true epitope surfaces, where antibodies recognize a well-defined conformation rather than a floppy loop.

The E101-like loop shows a more mixed pattern, which is interesting in a different way. Some positions tolerate alanine pretty well, while others produce strong destabilizing effects. This combination of some flexible spots paired with a few ridgid residues is common in exposed loops that participate in ligand or antibody interactions. A loop like this often has one or two anchor residues that help hold it in place, while the rest can shift or adapt slightly without collapsing the fold. The energetic sensitivity at specific positions supports the notion that this loop forms part of a structured, antibody-accessible patch on the ATD.

It’s also worth noting that charge swaps tend to be the most disruptive in both regions. This makes sense because many antibody epitopes rely on precise side-chain interactions and electrostatic complementarity. Changing the charge often changes the local surface chemistry enough to distort the native conformation, especially in areas that help define the ATD’s overall shape.

Taken together, the ΔΔG behavior across these two loops supports their interpretation as energetically meaningful, conformationally defined surfaces rather than flexible or low-importance segments. This matches what has been shown experimentally: antibodies targeting NR1 often rely on stable loop geometries within the ATD, including the regions around E101 and N368. The predicted energetic sensitivity adds another layer of evidence that these loops are likely to be authentic, ligandable epitope sites on NR1.
