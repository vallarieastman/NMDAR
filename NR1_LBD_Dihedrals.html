Structural Question 1: LBD Dihedral Shifts

φ/ψ Dihedral Analysis of NR1 Ligand-Binding Domain

Author: Vallari Eastman
Date: October 2025

Formal Structural Question 1:

What can phi (φ) and psi (ψ) dihedral angle analysis of the ligand-binding domain (LBD) reveal about conformational changes in NR1 between agonist- and antagonist-bound states?

Introduction:

The crystal structure of the NR1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) provides a detailed molecular framework for understanding how glycine binding drives NMDA receptor activation. Furukawa & Gouaux (2003) describe the structural organization of the NR1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) as composed of two discontinuous polypeptide segments: S1 (N-terminal portion) forming lobe D1 and S2 (C-terminal portion) forming lobe D2. Together, they fold into a hinged bilobed clamshell typical of ionotri=opic glutamate receptor LBDs. Upon agonist (glycine) binding, the D2 lobe rotates and moves toward D1, resulting in cleft closure that pulls on the transmembrane gating linkers, coupling ligand binding to pore opening (Furukawa & Gouaux, 2003). This cleft closure represents a key conformational change that transmits mechanical force via the S2–M3 linker to open the ion channel gate (Furukawa & Gouaux, 2003). Furukawa & Gouaux (2003) used the crystal structure PDB 1PBQ and noted that key residues forming the binding cleft include: D1 residues (Thr518, Arg523) form the rigid anchoring surface, holding glycine in place, D2 residues (Ser688, Thr690, Glu712, Asp732) form the mobile “closing” lobe, which moves toward D1 when glycine binds, and the resulting D1–D2 cleft closure is the mechanical trigger that initiates channel gating through tension on the S2–M3 linker (Figure 1).

Figure 1. Structural representation of the NR1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) showing the S1–S2 clamshell configuration (Furukawa & Gouaux, 2003). The GT linker (Gly–Thr linker) connects the LBD (S2 segment) to the transmembrane M3 helix, which forms the channel gate. When the D1–D2 cleft closes upon glycine binding, the D2 lobe moves toward D1, pulling on this GT linker. This tension is transmitted to the M3 helix, triggering channel opening. The disulfide (S–S) bonds stabilize the LBD’s tertiary structure and maintain the correct orientation of the D1 and D2 lobes. There are two key disulfide pairs: C420–C454 and C436–C455 (in D1) and C744–C798 (in D2). These S–S bonds act as structural scaffolds, ensuring the lobes retain rigidity where needed and that the hinge region (D1–D2 interface) can flex predictably without unfolding. Loop 1 (in Domain 1) connects secondary-structure elements (e.g., β-strands J and K) within D1. This helps stabilize the orientation of D1 relative to D2, contributing to the proper positioning of the glycine-binding pocket. Its conformational stability helps define the “rigid” half of the clamshell. Loop 2 (in Domain 2) is more flexible and directly involved in the hinge motion between D1 and D2. It plays a major role in closing the clamshell during agonist binding. Functionally, Loop 2 interacts with other receptor subunits (especially the GluN2 NTD α5 helix; Bleier et al., 2024), serving as an allosteric coupling site that tunes open probability (Po).

The ligand-binding domain (LBD) of the NMDA receptor subunit NR1 (GluN1) undergoes substantial conformational rearrangements during activation, transitioning from an open “apo” state to a closed “agonist-bound” state upon glycine and glutamate binding. Bleier and colleagues (2024) directly compared NR1/NR2A-D receptor conformations in apo-like, partially agonized, and fully agonized states using single-molecule FRET. They found that in the absence of agonists (apo state), the NR1/NR2B receptor adopts a loosely packed, flexible conformation that likely contributes to its lower open probability and slower gating (Bleier et al., 2024). When fully agonized (glycine + glutamate) conformation, all subtypes converge to a similar, compact NR1-LBD arrangement (Bleier et al., 2024). However, the NR1-NTD conformation remains subtype-specific, even when fully agonized, the NR1/NR2B NTD stays relatively open, suggesting that NTD separation correlates with its lower open probability (Bleier et al., 2024).

Figure 2. NR1 Subunit within the Tetramer:
The light grey ribbon represents one NR1 subunit, and the black ribbon represents its paired NR2 subunit within the heterodimer (Bleier et al., 2024). As two GluN1–GluN2 heterodimers form a dimer-of-dimers around the central ion pore, the NR1 subunit sits diagonally above NR2, and their ligand-binding domains (LBDs) and N-terminal domains (NTDs) are tightly coupled through multiple interfaces. D1–D2 describe the lobes of the ligand-binding clamshell (where the D1–D2 cleft closes upon agonist binding), while R1–R2 describe the lobes of the N-terminal modulatory domain (which can splay apart or compact depending on receptor subtype and allosteric modulation). The NTDs form the modulatory layer, while the LBDs form the ligand-binding and gating layer immediately above the transmembrane region. These two layers communicate mechanically - when ligands bind at the LBD, conformational strain propagates upward into the NTDs and downward to the channel gate.

When GluN2A is in apo and fully agonized states its confomrations look quite similar: the NTDs and LBDs are already compact at rest, consistent with high open probability (Po ~0.5). this indicates a receptor that’s “primed” for activation. For GluN2B, the NTDs are splayed open in the apo state and separate even further upon glutamate binding, while LBDs close normally. This decoupling between NTD and LBD explains its lower Po and distinct allosteric modulation. Both GluN2C / GluN2D exhibit very open, dynamic NTDs and LBDs in the apo state, which only compact upon full agonist binding. Reflects low Po, slow activation, and greater flexibility — consistent with their physiological role in tonic or extrasynaptic signaling.

Bleier and colleagues (2024) directly measured distances between NR1-LBD lobes (site D677) and found that agonist binding (glycine + glutamate) causes the LBDs to move closer together, consistent with D1–D2 cleft closure. Though the local backbone motions in the D1–D2 lobes (cleft closure) remain a universal gating step, the efficiency of mechanical coupling from that local motion to the pore varies with GluN2 subtype (Bleier et al., 2024). In NR1/NR2A receptors, ligand-induced closure of the GluN1 LBD transmits efficiently through the NTD–LBD interface to open the ion channel, resulting in a high open probability (Po ≈ 0.5), whereas in NR1/NR2B receptors, the LBDs still close upon agonist binding but the NTDs remain more splayed, weakening interdomain coupling and producing a lower open probability (Bleier et al., 2024).

A lower open probability (Po) means the NMDA receptor channel spends less time in the open, ion-conducting state, even when both agonists (glycine and glutamate) are bound. For NR1/NR2B, this has several physiological and pharmacological implications including slower channel gating kinetics, prolonged but weaker synaptic currents, and greater susceptibility to modulation by allosteric regulators such as zinc, protons, and ifenprodil (Bleier et al., 2024). Functionally, this allows NR1/NR2B receptors to shape slower, more sustained calcium signaling that supports processes like synaptic plasticity and developmental refinement, while pharmacologically making them a prime target for subtype-selective inhibitors that exploit their unique, low-Po conformational landscape.

This hinge region represents a structural “control point” where small molecules, antibodies, or allosteric modulators could stabilize specific conformations (open, closed, or intermediate). By identifying residues whose backbone geometry changes most dramatically between states, we can pinpoint flexible or energetically sensitive sites that define the LBD’s conformational energy landscape. These hinge and loop residues, therefore, may serve as allosteric drug-binding hotspots, allowing selective modulation of NR1 activity.

Building on these structural and conformational findings, I hypothesize that hinge and loop residues at the D1–D2 interface of the NR1 ligand-binding domain exhibit distinct φ/ψ angle clusters between apo and agonist-bound states. These backbone torsion differences are expected to reflect the clamshell-like opening and closing motion that underlies ligand-induced LBD rearrangement and mechanical coupling to the channel gate. Mapping φ/ψ transitions at the D1–D2 interface helps reveal where and how NR1 can be conformationally “tuned,” a key step in evaluating its structural and functional druggability.

Hypothesis:

Hinge and loop residues at the D1–D2 interface exhibit distinct φ/ψ angle clusters between apo and agonist-bound states are consistent with clamshell opening/closing.

Null Hypothesis:

φ/ψ differences are uniform/random across the LBD (no interface clustering), with no association to D1–D2 closure metrics.

Methods:

Sequence Used: PDB 7EU8 (human NR1 LBD, chains A & C) and PDB 4KCC (rat NR1 LBD, chain A)

The agonist-bound conformation of the GluN1 ligand-binding domain (LBD) was extracted from chains A and C of the full-length human GluN1/GluN2B NMDA receptor (PDB 7EU8), which was solved with glycine and glutamate bound. The apo (unliganded) reference was represented by the rat GluN1 LBD (PDB 4KCC, chain A) in the open-cleft state. Human and rat NR1 share ~99% sequence identity across the LBD, particularly within the hinge and loop regions, validating 4KCC as a model for the unbound state.

φ (phi) and ψ (psi) backbone dihedral angles were computed directly from the atomic coordinates using Biopython’s PPBuilder().build_peptides() and get_phi_psi_list() functions. Each dihedral was defined geometrically by four consecutive backbone atoms—C(i–1)–N(i)–Cα(i)–C(i) for φ and N(i)–Cα(i)–C(i)–N(i+1) for ψ—and returned in degrees within the range (–180°, +180°]. Residues lacking complete backbone atoms were excluded. For each structure, a sequence of angle-defined residues was generated, filtered for valid φ/ψ pairs, and converted into a one-letter sequence representation.

To ensure residue-level correspondence, the angle-available sequences from 7EU8 and 4KCC were globally aligned using Biopython’s PairwiseAligner with the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix (gap open = –10, extend = –0.5). The resulting alignment was used to pair residues one-to-one, reconstructing gapped sequences robustly across Biopython versions. For each aligned residue pair, Δφ and Δψ were computed as circular differences wrapped into (–180°, +180°], and an average deviation metric (|Δ| mean = (|Δφ| + |Δψ|)/2) was calculated to quantify backbone rearrangement.

The resulting dataset (NR1_phi_psi_comparison_ANGLE_ALIGNED.csv) contains one row per aligned residue pair, reporting the bound and apo φ/ψ values, the angular differences, and region assignments (S1, S2, hinge, or loop). These values were used to identify localized backbone motions associated with ligand-induced clamshell closure of the NR1 LBD.

How φ and ψ Dihedral Angles Were Computed

For each residue in the NR1 ligand-binding domain (LBD), the φ (phi) and ψ (psi) backbone dihedral angles were computed directly from the atomic coordinates in the PDB structures using Biopython’s PPBuilder().build_peptides() and get_phi_psi_list() functions.

Each angle is defined geometrically from four sequential backbone atoms:

Angle Rotation Bond Atoms Used
φ (phi) N–Cα C(i–1) – N(i) – Cα(i) – C(i)
ψ (psi) Cα–C N(i) – Cα(i) – C(i) – N(i+1)

Why This Matters

The φ/ψ angles describe local backbone conformation, the geometric “fold” at each residue. By comparing φ/ψ between the apo (4KCC) and agonist-bound (7EU8) NR1 structures, conformational changes that underlie hinge motion during receptor activation can be quantified.

The dihedral angle is calculated as the torsion between two planes:

θ = atan2((b₂u · (n₁ × n₂)), (n₁ · n₂))

where b₀, b₁, b₂ are the bond vectors connecting the four atoms. The result is reported in degrees, ranging from −180° to +180°.

# ---------- Helpers ----------

def angle_seq(df):
    """Return (df_filtered, seq_string) for residues with non-null φ/ψ."""
    df2 = df[pd.notna(df["phi_deg"]) & pd.notna(df["psi_deg"])].reset_index(drop=True)
    seq = "".join(df2["aa"].tolist())
    return df2, seq

def circular_diff_deg(a, b):
    """Smallest signed diff (deg) in (-180, 180]; safe on NaN."""
    if pd.isna(a) or pd.isna(b):
        return np.nan
    d = (a - b + 180.0) % 360.0 - 180.0
    return 180.0 if d == -180.0 else d

def build_gapped_robust(aln, s1, s2):
    """
    Return (gapped_s1, gapped_s2) robustly across Biopython versions.
    Prefers aln.path; falls back to aln.aligned; lastly tries seqA/seqB or slicing.
    """
    # Preferred: stepwise path
    if hasattr(aln, "path") and aln.path is not None:
        g1, g2 = [], []
        path = aln.path
        for (i1, j1), (i2, j2) in zip(path[:-1], path[1:]):
            di, dj = i2 - i1, j2 - j1
            if di == 1 and dj == 1:
                g1.append(s1[i1]); g2.append(s2[j1])
            elif di == 1 and dj == 0:
                g1.append(s1[i1]); g2.append('-')
            elif di == 0 and dj == 1:
                g1.append('-');    g2.append(s2[j1])
            else:
                step = min(di, dj)
                for k in range(step):
                    g1.append(s1[i1+k]); g2.append(s2[j1+k])
                for k in range(di - step):
                    g1.append(s1[i1+step+k]); g2.append('-')
                for k in range(dj - step):
                    g1.append('-'); g2.append(s2[j1+step+k])
        return "".join(g1), "".join(g2)

    # Fallback: block coordinates
    if hasattr(aln, "aligned") and aln.aligned is not None:
        blocks1, blocks2 = aln.aligned  # lists of (start,end) for each sequence
        g1, g2 = [], []
        i = j = 0
        for (s1_start, s1_end), (s2_start, s2_end) in zip(blocks1, blocks2):
            if s1_start > i:
                for k in range(i, s1_start):
                    g1.append(s1[k]); g2.append('-')
                i = s1_start
            if s2_start > j:
                for k in range(j, s2_start):
                    g1.append('-'); g2.append(s2[k])
                j = s2_start
            n = s1_end - s1_start
            for k in range(n):
                g1.append(s1[s1_start+k]); g2.append(s2[s2_start+k])
            i = s1_end; j = s2_end
        if i < len(s1):
            for k in range(i, len(s1)):
                g1.append(s1[k]); g2.append('-')
        if j < len(s2):
            for k in range(j, len(s2)):
                g1.append('-'); g2.append(s2[k])
        return "".join(g1), "".join(g2)

    # Last-ditch conveniences
    if hasattr(aln, "seqA") and hasattr(aln, "seqB"):
        return str(aln.seqA), str(aln.seqB)
    try:
        a, b = aln[:2]
        return str(a), str(b)
    except Exception:
        pass

    raise AttributeError("Alignment lacks .path/.aligned; cannot rebuild gapped strings safely.")

Analysis

Localized Hinge Flexibility

The φ/ψ angle comparisons reveal that glycine binding does not trigger widespread rearrangement of the NR1 ligand-binding domain (LBD). Instead, conformational change is concentrated at the S2 hinge, where the D2 lobe pivots toward the rigid D1 lobe. This localized motion supports the clamshell closure model proposed by Furukawa & Gouaux (2003), in which D2 rotation closes the glycine-binding cleft and transmits force to the transmembrane gate through the GT linker. The distribution of φ/ψ differences indicates that most residues maintain stable geometry.

The S1 lobe remains largely static while the S2 lobe undergoes most of the torsional change. This asymmetry allows one domain to serve as a mechanical anchor and the other as the moving lever, converting small local rotations into channel gating. This means thatligand binding is a precise motion rather than random structural fluctuations. The residues showing the greatest φ/ψ shifts including Trp731, Tyr795, Phe753, Arg663, and Glu661, suggest this is the mechanical axis of the LBD. Because these residues experience the largest angular changes, they are probable sites of energetic sensitivity. The hinge and nearby loops identified as flexible regions define potential allosteric binding hotspots. Molecules or antibodies that stabilize the open (apo-like) or closed (agonized) hinge conformations could bias receptor gating without interfering with the orthosteric ligand site. Such approaches may allow subtype-selective tuning of NMDA receptor activity, offering a path toward therapeutics that modulate receptor kinetics with high precision.

Overall, this analysis links local atomic-scale motion to global receptor function. The hinge flexibility quantified here provides the physical mechanism underlying clamshell closure and explains subtype-specific gating behavior. The NR1 LBD is rigid enough to preserve structure and still flexible enough for ligand binding.

Future Directions

Antagonist Conformation

Building on these findings, the next phase of this analysis could focus on extending the φ/ψ comparison beyond the agonist-bound (7EU8) and apo (4KCC) states to include the antagonist-bound conformation. This will allow direct comparison of how glycine binding versus inhibitory ligand binding differentially stabilize specific backbone geometries. By quantifying φ/ψ distributions across all three states, it will be possible to identify residues that toggle between discrete conformational clusters depending on ligand type.

Continuous Backbone Representation

In parallel, generating a continuous backbone representation of φ/ψ transitions will enable visualization of the LBD’s conformational trajectory along the activation pathway. Rather than discrete static comparisons, this approach would interpolate torsion angles along the sequence to produce a smooth, residue-by-residue map of structural displacement. Such a representation could be further integrated with molecular dynamics simulations or principal component analysis (PCA) to capture the full spectrum of hinge flexibility and coupling between D1 and D2 lobes.

Implications for allosteric modulation and drug design

Together, these future directions could refine our understanding of how different ligands sculpt the NR1 conformational landscape. Antagonist-bound comparisons can reveal which hinge or loop residues are “frozen” in an open configuration, while continuous φ/ψ mapping will characterize the full mechanical range available to the LBD. This integrated structural framework could ultimately guide structure-based drug design by pinpointing residues whose backbone dynamics directly determine the receptor’s gating efficiency and pharmacological profile.
